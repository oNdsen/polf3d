# How POLF 3D was built

This page is the project documentation of POLF 3D: how it came about, why it grew the way it did, and what it is really about.

## What this project is about

POLF 3D is a fun project. Nothing more, and that is the whole point.

There is one question behind it: **how far can you push PowerShell?** PowerShell is a language for administration and automation. Nobody would pick it to write a first-person shooter, and that is exactly why it is fun to try. There is no product behind this, no business plan and nothing to prove. It exists because it is a joy to find out what is possible, and because a 90s shooter that runs from a `.ps1` file makes people smile.

## Where it started

id Software released the source code of their 1992 shooter years ago. The first step was to read it and write down, in our own words, how such a game works: ray casting, doors, the enemies' state machines, the map format, weapons and items. The second step was to build a new game from that description alone, with own code, own maps, own names and own art. Nothing of the original is in this repository.

The code was written with an AI assistant (Claude Code). I set the direction and the rules, played every build, reported what was broken and decided what stays. The rules from the first hour on:

- as much PowerShell as possible, as little C# as necessary
- every function uses an approved verb, internal helpers included
- no asset files: every texture, sprite, sound and tune is generated by code
- one commit per feature, and nothing is pushed before the tests are green

## The first version

The first commit was already a complete little game: a textured ray caster, doors, keys, secret walls, a lift, guards driven by a table of states, a few weapons, five floors, saved games. Beside it the tools that are still there today: a level generator, a map validator, the verb check and a headless self-test.

It ran at a playable frame rate, and that answered the first question: yes, PowerShell can do this.

But it played very much like its godfather. Own code and own art, yet no idea of its own. That was not what I wanted. POLF 3D should stand on its own feet, with a motto of its own, and still remind everybody of the 90s classic the moment they see it.

## Why it kept growing

From there on every round had the same shape: play, notice what is missing, ask "does PowerShell manage that too?", build it, test it, play again. I never had a feature list. I had curiosity, and after every round the answer was "that worked, so let us drill deeper and see where the limits are".

**First it had to feel right.** A shooter lives from feedback: music, hits you can see and hear, light, fog, a world that reacts. None of that is needed to prove a ray caster works. All of it is needed for a game you want to keep playing.

**Then it needed more game.** More kinds of enemies and weapons, sneaking, levers, traps, secrets. Not to tick boxes, but because every new mechanism was another small test of the language: can the AI hear noise through open doors, can a wall come down, can you look through a window?

**Then came the things around a game.** A level editor, demos, game pads, a soundtrack, network play. These were the "surely not in PowerShell" items, and that made them the most tempting ones.

**And then it found its motto.** This is where it became its own game. The question was no longer "what did shooters of that time have?" but "what can only a shooter written in PowerShell have?". You are the admin on call. The building is full of consoles, error screens and racks. Your powers are named after the common parameters: `-WhatIf` shows you the next two seconds, `-Confirm` takes them back. There is a real, sandboxed PowerShell console inside the game, and `Get-Enemy | Stop-Enemy` does what you think it does. Every floor is a Pester-style test suite. The alarm level of the building is its execution policy. From that point on every new idea had to pass one check: does it put PowerShell in the foreground?

**1.0.0** was the moment it stopped being a folder of scripts and became something you can download, start with a double click and play through. Since then it grows the same way as before, only that the ideas and bug reports now also come from the people who play it.

## Where the limits are

What we found out on the way:

- **Per-pixel loops are the one thing PowerShell cannot do fast enough.** Scaling the picture to the window, filling wall columns and floors, and the character output of terminal mode are C#. So are the audio callback, which has to answer in real time, and the XInput declarations. That is all of the C#: four small files, compiled once and then loaded from a cache.
- **Everything else is PowerShell** and holds up: walking the rays through the grid, the enemies' AI, line of sight and sound propagation, the network protocol, the composer, the dungeon generator, the GIF encoder.
- **.NET is the lever.** A note of music is one wave pattern copied with `Buffer.BlockCopy`, and the channels are added up with `System.Numerics.Vector`; half a minute of music renders in under a second. Presenting through `BufferedGraphics` instead of drawing a bitmap was the difference between 30 and 55 fps.
- **JSON is fine for saved games and far too slow for Undo.** The powers use in-memory snapshots that clone arrays and copy living actors field by field, which takes a few milliseconds.
- **The language has traps.** Arrays unroll when you do not expect it, and `[Array]::Sort(keys, items)` leaves an `object[]` of items alone, so for a while every enemy was drawn over every column. Each of those is now pinned down by a self-test.
- **The console's sandbox is so empty that PowerShell's own tab completion finds nothing in it.** The game has its own completer, which in return knows the enemies on the floor and the files on the terminal.
- **Start-up is the price of having no asset files.** Everything is generated when the game starts. Music and voices are cached, and the soundtrack is composed in a background runspace.

## How a change gets in

Every push runs the headless self-test, the map validator, the verb check and a playback of the recorded attract demo, which has to end exactly as it did when it was recorded. A bot plays the floors on every difficulty to give a first idea of the balance. After that it is played by hand, which is still how most bugs are found.

The limit has not been reached yet. Ideas and bug reports are welcome in the [issues](../../issues).
